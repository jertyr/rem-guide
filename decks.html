Decks

Decks are outdoor structures built with pressure-treated framing and a variety of surface materials. The foundation is typically post hole footings, the structure follows standard framing principles, and the surface can be wood, composite, or PVC. Railings are required when the deck is over 30" high.

Footings

Deck footings are post holes dug below frost line and filled with concrete. In Michigan, that's 42" deep minimum (48" in northern counties). Holes should be 12" diameter for most residential decks. Use post base brackets rather than embedding posts directly in concrete to prevent rot. See the Footings section for more details on post hole footings.

Framing

Deck framing uses pressure-treated lumber. Use ground contact rated (UC4) for posts and beams, and above ground rated (UC3B) for joists and blocking. Always use fasteners rated for PT lumber - stainless steel or hot-dipped galvanized. Standard fasteners will corrode.

Ledger Board: Must be properly flashed and bolted to the house structure, not just the siding. Remove siding where the ledger attaches and install flashing above to prevent water damage.

Joist Spacing: Composite decking typically requires 16" on center or less. Check manufacturer specs.

Decking Material Options

The biggest decision in deck construction is the surface material. Treated lumber is the most economical option. Composite and PVC materials cost more upfront but need minimal maintenance and last longer.

Railings

Railings are required for decks over 30" high. Code requires 36" minimum height and balusters spaced no more than 4" apart.

Railing Options

  • Wood: $25-45/LF. Cheapest option, requires staining/painting every few years
  • Aluminum (Westbury/Fortress): $75-130/LF. No maintenance, clean modern look, available with glass or cable infill
  • Composite (TimberTech/Trex): $60-110/LF. Matches composite decking, minimal maintenance, variety of colors

Process

  1. Layout and dig post holes to frost depth (42"-48" in Michigan), 12" diameter
  2. Get footing inspection
  3. Pour concrete footings with post base brackets
  4. Install posts and beams (ground contact PT lumber)
  5. Attach ledger board to house (remove siding, flash properly, bolt to rim joist)
  6. Install joists and blocking (PT lumber, 16" OC or less for composite)
  7. Apply joist tape to protect tops of joists
  8. Install decking boards (space 1/4" for composite, 1/8" for wood)
  9. Install fascia to cover rim joists
  10. Build railings if deck is over 30" high
  11. Get final inspection

Best Practices

  • Always use PT-rated fasteners (stainless or galvanized). Standard screws will corrode.
  • Never attach ledger through siding. Remove siding and flash properly.
  • Use post base brackets, not embedded posts (prevents rot)
  • Slope deck 1/8" per foot away from house for drainage
  • Check composite decking manufacturer specs for joist spacing (often 16" or 12" OC)
  • Apply joist tape to tops of all joists before decking

Maintenance

Composite and PVC

  • Rinse annually with garden hose; wash with mild soap and water for stubborn dirt
  • No staining, sealing, or sanding required
  • Avoid wire brushes — they scratch the cap layer

IPE and Tropical Hardwood

IPE is one of the most durable decking materials available, but it requires active maintenance. After the first PNW fall/winter (or any wet climate), the deck will grey and develop a layer of algae and mildew — enough to make the surface slick. Two valid paths:

  • Let it silver: After cleaning, IPE will naturally grey out. When spring/summer sun hits the surface it turns a silver-grey that looks great and requires no staining
  • Restore the color: Annual staining brings it back to the rich warm hue it had when new — plan for a full day project each year

Annual Cleaning Process

A pressure washer wand alone leaves marks and takes hours. The process below produces a clean, even result in a fraction of the time.

  • Step 1 — Apply cleaning solution
    • Clear the deck and lightly mist the surface with water
    • Mix: 2 cups OxiClean + 1 cup Borax + ½ cup Dawn dish soap in 2 gallons of water
    • Apply liberally with a moderately stiff push brush, covering the entire surface
    • Let sit at least 30 minutes — the solution needs time to break down algae and mildew
  • Step 2 — Pressure wash with a surface cleaner
    • Use a surface cleaning attachment, not a wand — eliminates streaks and cuts time by ~75%
    • Move slowly and evenly over the entire deck
    • If using a wand, use a 40-degree nozzle; expect wand marks and significantly more time
  • Step 3 — Apply brightener
    • Mix: 1 cup oxalic acid in 2 gallons of water
    • Apply liberally, wait 30 minutes, rinse thoroughly
    • Wear gloves and eye protection — oxalic acid is a skin and eye irritant; read product precautions before use

After the brightener step, the deck looks great and can stand on its own without staining.

Staining (Optional)

  • Wait 48 hours of dry weather after cleaning before applying stain
  • Use a transparent penetrating oil stain — Sikkens is the recommended product for IPE, Tigerwood, Ironwood, and similar tropical hardwoods
  • Avoid film-forming stains — they peel on hardwoods
  • Expect to repeat annually in wet or shaded climates

Inspections

Most jurisdictions require a footing inspection before pouring concrete and a final inspection after completion. The final inspection will check railing height (36" minimum), baluster spacing (4" maximum), and proper ledger attachment.
