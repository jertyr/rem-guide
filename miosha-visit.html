On site

What the visit looked like

How it went

  • No notice. A compliance officer arrived and asked us to put our tools down.
  • The inspection started immediately.
  • He stayed an hour or two: making notes, interviewing carpenters and taking photos.
  • He asked for documentation: certifications, training records and our safety program.

Who was there

A lead carpenter, an intermediate carpenter and a junior carpenter.

What we were doing

  • Cutting fiber cement with an angle grinder and HEPA vac
  • A drywall patch, done outside
  • A miter saw set up on site
The citations

What we were cited for

ItemTypeWhat they foundHow we fixed it
Citation 1, Item 1aSeriousWorker without eye or face protectionSafety glasses required and stocked in every vehicle
Citation 1, Item 1bSeriousNo proof of a First Aid / CPR trained person on site. Our lead was certified, but the card was on his phone.Certification on file; a second person trained June 17
Citation 2, Item 1Other than seriousLadder safety trainingCrew ladder training held July 2
Citation 2, Item 3Other than seriousNo silica training in our hazard communication documentsSilica exposure control plan and training added July 1

Also discussed: our hazard communication program was out of date. It still said MSDS, which have been SDS for over a decade, and there were no SDS on site.

The penalty

The fine may be small the first time

$900

Proposed penalty in the citation package

$450

After the 50% Penalty Reduction Agreement

$9,000

Repeat citations can carry 10x the original

The clock

How much time you have to comply

15 days

Working days from receiving the citations to sign the Penalty Reduction Agreement (PRA) or file an appeal. Ask early; MIOSHA prefers within 5.

Fix first

Construction citations must be corrected before MIOSHA will approve the PRA.

15 days

Working days after approval to pay. Miss it and the full original penalty is due.

3 days

Working days the approved agreement stays posted, or until everything is corrected.

Our dates

June 23 citations issued · July 1 and 2 training done and plan rewritten · July 6 PRA and proof sent · July 13 approved · July 22 paid (due August 3)

Signing a PRA means accepting the citations and waiving the right to contest. Source: MIOSHA Agency Instruction COM-04 and our signed agreement. Our experience, not legal advice.

1 · The written program

We updated our safety plan

From a 3-ring binder to an electronic document we can all access.

Before: the 2016 binder

  • Old office address, committee of people long gone
  • Generic "sample" language, few standards cited
  • No silica plan, no respirator program
  • Still called them MSDS
  • Nobody knew where it was

After: revised June 2026

  • 18 sections, each citing the OSHA 1926 or MIOSHA standard
  • Competent person named: PM, then lead carpenter or mason
  • Silica exposure control plan built on Table 1 tasks
  • Respiratory protection and a fit test protocol
  • "Training point" boxes that double as toolbox talks
  • Forms: job safety analysis, site inspection, incident report, training sign-off, emergency posting

3 · Gear where the work is

A safety kit in every work vehicle

  • Safety glasses (Z87)
  • Hard hat
  • Hearing protection
  • N95 dust masks
  • P100 respirator
  • Work gloves
  • Safety boots
  • Hi-vis vest
  • Voltage pen tester
  • First aid kit
  • Fire extinguisher (2A)
  • GFCI cord

How we run it

  • Checklist by vehicle number, so we know who is missing what
  • We buy whatever is missing
  • On bigger jobs, a red safety bag stays on site
  • We are responsible for everyone on our sites, subs included

Takeaways

What I'd tell another remodeler

  1. Proof has to be on site. A cert at the office or buried in a phone didn't count for us.
  2. Name a competent person on every job and make sure they know it.
  3. Get out your written plan and look it over. Ours was 10 years old.
  4. A toolbox talk is the cheapest proof you have of an active program. Do them, and document them as a daily log.
  5. Put together red safety bags. Stock one for every truck and leave one on your bigger jobs.
  6. As a contractor you are responsible for the safety of everyone else on your job site.
