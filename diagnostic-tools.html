Diagnostic Tools for Builders

Using thermal imaging, moisture metering, in-wall sensors, and air-quality meters to find problems — and prove the fix worked.

Jerry Tyrrell · Dexter Builders

Introduction

Why diagnostic tools belong on a remodeler's truck

Most callbacks and warranty issues live behind a finished surface. The right meter or imager turns a guess into a measurement — we stop opening drywall to "go look," and we can prove a repair worked instead of hoping it did. Each of the tools shown here paid for itself on a single job.

I've brought every one of these tools with me today, so we'll handle them and run a few of them live. Then we'll walk through three jobs — Maynard, Gustafson, and McGrath — that show the same toolkit applied to three different problems: a quick diagnosis, a stubborn one the first fix didn't solve, and a year-long verification before we closed up a wall.

Tool 1

Thermal imaging camera

A handheld infrared camera reads surface temperature across a whole wall at once. It does not see through drywall, but it shows the thermal footprint of what is behind it — cold pipes, missing insulation, air leaks, wet drywall as it evaporates. Inside a heated building in winter, a frozen water line shows up as a clear cold streak.

Tool 2

In-wall temperature sensors

Where the thermal camera gives a snapshot, these give a graph. Small data loggers drop into a drilled hole and record temperature continuously — how cold did it actually get overnight, and did adding insulation or rerouting the line bring the cavity back into a safe range. We leave them in place for hours, days, or in some cases a full season.

Make / Model Onset HOBO Temp logger
Logging Continuous; configurable interval
Best use Verifying a fix, capturing overnight lows, long-duration monitoring
Limits Point measurement; needs a small drilled hole

This is the highest-leverage tool on the truck. A thermal camera can find a cold spot, but only a logger can tell you whether the fix you just made will hold up at 2 a.m. on the coldest night of the year.

Tool 3

Pin-style moisture meter

Two metal pins press into the substrate and read electrical resistance. Cheap, accurate, and unambiguous — you get a real moisture-content number. The tradeoff: the pins leave two small holes, so it's a tool for unfinished surfaces, framing inspections, or hidden locations.

Make / Model Kobalt pin moisture meter
Reading % moisture content
Best use Framing, sheathing, subfloor, baseboards — anywhere the marks won't matter
Limits Leaves pin marks; not for finished, visible surfaces
Tool 4

Contact-free moisture meter

A non-invasive scanner reads moisture through a finished surface using radio-frequency or capacitance — no pin holes, no marks. It's the right tool for diagnosing trouble in a finished home where we cannot leave evidence behind. It reads relative moisture, so we use it to find the wettest spot first, then confirm with the pin meter wherever a hidden location lets us.

Tool 5

CO2 meter

Carbon-dioxide concentration is the cleanest, simplest proxy for ventilation performance in a home. Outdoor air sits around 420 ppm. Inside a tight, occupied house with poor ventilation we can see numbers north of 1,500 ppm — the kind of levels that show up as headaches and bad sleep before anyone calls a builder. As we build tighter envelopes, this is the meter that proves the mechanical ventilation is doing what the design says it should.

Tool 6

Radon detector

Radon is invisible, odorless, and the second-leading cause of lung cancer in the U.S. The EPA action level is 4.0 pCi/L. A continuous radon monitor gives both a long-term running average and a short-term reading, so we can tell the difference between a normal daily swing and a real problem — and verify a mitigation system after install.

Make / Model CRADTEC continuous radon monitor
Reading pCi/L (short and long-term averages)
Action level EPA: 4.0 pCi/L
Best use Pre/post mitigation, basement/crawl monitoring, peace of mind on a finished basement
Case Study 1

Maynard — frozen pipe

The call: a toilet wouldn't fill.

What started as a routine "toilet won't refill" service call turned into a textbook diagnostic. The supply line was running through an exterior wall and had frozen. The thermal camera identified the cold zone in seconds. An in-wall sensor confirmed the cavity temperature directly — and after the fix, verified the line stayed in a safe range overnight.

Case Study 3

McGrath — long-term monitoring

The situation: a water line in an exterior wall that we weren't sure would survive winter.

Sometimes the right answer is to wait and watch. Rather than tear open a finished wall on a hunch, we instrumented a questionable water line and let it run through a full heating season. About a year of data gave us a defensible answer — either the assembly performs, or it doesn't, and we have the graph to prove which.
