Decks

Quick Reference / TLDR

  • Footings: Post hole style footings below frost line (42"-48" in Michigan), 12" diameter minimum
  • Framing: Pressure-treated lumber - use ground contact rated for posts/beams, standard ACQ for joists/blocking
  • Ledger Board: Must be properly flashed and attached to house structure (not just siding)
  • Decking Material: Options range from treated lumber ($0.81/LF) to premium composites ($7.57/LF)
  • Railing: 36" minimum height; options include wood, aluminum (Westbury/Fortress), or composite (TimberTech/Trex)
  • Fasteners: Use stainless steel or approved coated fasteners for all connections
  • Inspection: Footing inspection before pouring concrete, final inspection after completion

Overview

Deck construction combines structural engineering with aesthetic choices to create an outdoor living space. While the basic framing principles are well-established, material selection for decking surfaces and railings has expanded dramatically in recent years. This guide focuses on the key components, foundation systems, and the wide range of decking and railing options available today.

Deck Components

Structural Elements

  • Footings: Concrete piers extending below frost line to prevent frost heave
  • Posts: Vertical support members, typically 6x6 pressure-treated lumber
  • Beams: Horizontal members that span between posts, support joists
  • Ledger Board: Attached to house, supports one end of joists
  • Joists: Horizontal framing members that support decking, typically 2x8 or 2x10
  • Blocking: Cross-bracing between joists for stability
  • Rim Joist: Outer joist that frames the perimeter

Surface and Safety Elements

  • Decking Boards: Surface walking material (see detailed options below)
  • Fascia: Covers rim joist and structural framing
  • Railings: Guard rails required for decks over 30" high
  • Stairs: Access from deck to ground level
  • Post Caps: Protective and decorative caps for railing posts

Footings and Foundation

Post Hole Style Footings

Post hole footings are the most common foundation system for residential decks. They provide adequate support while being relatively straightforward to install.

Footing Requirements:

  • Depth: Below frost line - 42" minimum in Michigan (48" in some northern counties)
  • Diameter: 12" minimum for most residential decks; 16"-18" for larger/higher decks
  • Spacing: Determined by beam span and load calculations, typically 6'-10' apart
  • Concrete: 3000 PSI minimum, use concrete mix designed for footings

Installation Process:

  1. Locate and mark footing positions per plan
  2. Dig holes using post hole digger or auger
  3. Place cardboard tube forms (Sonotube) if required by code
  4. Set post base hardware or embedded post brackets before concrete sets
  5. Pour concrete and ensure proper alignment
  6. Allow concrete to cure (typically 7 days before loading)

Post Base Options:

  • Embedded Post: Post set directly in concrete (traditional but not recommended - prone to rot)
  • Post Base Brackets: Metal bracket anchored to footing, post bolts to bracket (preferred method)
  • Adjustable Post Bases: Allow for height adjustment and better drainage

Treated Lumber Options

Pressure-Treated Lumber Types

Ground Contact Rated (UC4A/UC4B)

  • Use: Posts, beams, joists within 6" of ground, any wood in direct ground contact
  • Treatment Level: Higher chemical retention (0.40-0.60 PCF for ACQ)
  • Color: Typically darker green/brown due to higher treatment levels
  • Required For: Posts on footings, bottom beams, any framing touching concrete

Above Ground Rated (UC3B)

  • Use: Joists, blocking, rim joists, ledger boards (when properly flashed)
  • Treatment Level: Lower chemical retention (0.25 PCF for ACQ)
  • Color: Lighter green tint
  • Cost: Typically 10-15% less expensive than ground contact rated

Treatment Chemical Types:

  • ACQ (Alkaline Copper Quaternary): Most common, copper-based, requires specific fasteners
  • CA-C (Copper Azole): Lower copper content, less corrosive to fasteners
  • MCQ (Micronized Copper Quaternary): Newer treatment, deeper penetration, cleaner appearance
Important Note: Always use fasteners rated for pressure-treated lumber. Standard fasteners will corrode rapidly. Use stainless steel, hot-dipped galvanized, or proprietary coated fasteners approved for PT lumber contact.

Decking Surface Options

Deck surface material is one of the most important decisions in deck construction, affecting appearance, maintenance, longevity, and cost. Options range from traditional treated lumber to premium composite and PVC materials.

Material Categories

1. Treated Lumber Decking

  • Material: Pressure-treated pine, typically 5/4 x 6 boards
  • Cost: $0.81 - $5.99 per linear foot
  • Lifespan: 15-50 years depending on quality and maintenance
  • Maintenance: Annual cleaning, staining/sealing every 2-3 years
  • Pros: Lowest cost, traditional appearance, can be stained any color
  • Cons: Requires regular maintenance, prone to warping/splitting, splinters

2. Cedar Decking

  • Material: Natural cedar, 5/4 x 6 boards
  • Cost: $3.33 per linear foot (approx.)
  • Lifespan: 15-20 years with maintenance
  • Maintenance: Annual cleaning, sealing recommended
  • Pros: Natural beauty, aromatic, naturally rot-resistant
  • Cons: Expensive, weathers to gray without sealer, softer wood prone to dents

3. Composite Decking (85% Recycled)

  • Material: Wood fiber and plastic blend with protective cap
  • Cost: $3.38 - $7.22 per linear foot
  • Lifespan: 25-30 years
  • Maintenance: Minimal - periodic cleaning only
  • Pros: Low maintenance, won't rot/warp, variety of colors, environmentally friendly
  • Cons: Can fade over time, hot underfoot in direct sun, higher initial cost

4. Advanced PVC (Polymer) Decking

  • Material: 100% synthetic polymer with cap layer
  • Cost: $6.53 - $7.57 per linear foot
  • Lifespan: 50+ years
  • Maintenance: Minimal - cleaning only, no sealing required
  • Pros: Most durable, superior stain resistance, won't fade/rot/crack, widest color range
  • Cons: Highest cost, can expand/contract with temperature changes

Key Features Explained:
  • 4-Sided Cap: Complete protection on all sides, best moisture resistance
  • 3-Sided Cap: Capped on top and sides, exposed composite on bottom (for hidden fastener systems)
  • Solid/Flat Profile: Smooth, flat surface
  • Scalloped/Fluted Profile: Textured or grooved surface, more natural wood appearance
  • Plug Screw Compatible (YES): Can use face-mounted screws with color-matched plugs
  • Plug Screw Compatible (NO): Requires hidden fastener system

Hidden Fastener Systems

Most composite and PVC decking can be installed with hidden fasteners that clip to the side of boards, eliminating visible screws on the deck surface. Benefits include:

  • Cleaner appearance without screw heads
  • No standing water in screw holes
  • Easier board removal for repairs
  • Typically adds $0.50-1.00 per square foot to material cost

Railing Systems

Railings are required by code for any deck over 30" above grade. Modern railing systems offer a wide range of aesthetic and functional options.

Wood Railings

  • Material: Pressure-treated, cedar, or composite lumber
  • Cost: $25-45 per linear foot (materials only)
  • Design: Traditional balusters, horizontal boards, or decorative patterns
  • Pros: Lowest cost, customizable, traditional appearance, easy repairs
  • Cons: Requires maintenance (staining/painting), balusters can loosen over time
  • Best For: Budget-conscious projects, matching existing wood aesthetics

Aluminum Railing Systems

Aluminum railings offer a clean, modern look with minimal maintenance. Two leading brands:

Westbury Aluminum Railings

  • Cost: $75-120 per linear foot
  • Profiles: Traditional, Contemporary, Estate, Tuscany
  • Colors: Black, white, bronze, clay (powder-coated finish)
  • Infill Options: Aluminum balusters, glass panels, decorative panels
  • Features: Pre-assembled rail sections, adjustable for slopes, powder-coated finish, lifetime warranty
  • Popular For: Clean lines, wide color selection, glass panel compatibility

Fortress Aluminum Railings

  • Cost: $85-130 per linear foot
  • Profiles: AL13 (modern), Infinity, Evolution, Pure View (glass)
  • Colors: Black, white, bronze, sand (premium finishes available)
  • Infill Options: Aluminum balusters, cable rail, glass panels
  • Features: Heavy-duty construction, cable rail option, glass rail system, lifetime warranty
  • Popular For: Cable rail systems (FE26), frameless glass options, modern aesthetic

Aluminum Railing Pros/Cons:

Pros:

  • No maintenance - won't rust or rot
  • Clean, modern appearance
  • Doesn't obstruct views (especially with glass or cable infill)
  • Available in pre-assembled sections for easier installation
  • Lifetime warranties common

Cons:

  • Higher initial cost
  • Limited color options compared to paint
  • Requires level mounting surface
  • Can dent (though quality brands use thicker gauge aluminum)

Composite Railing Systems

Composite railings match composite decking materials, providing a cohesive look with minimal maintenance.

TimberTech Railing

  • Cost: $65-110 per linear foot
  • Collections: RadianceRail (composite posts, aluminum balusters), Impression Rail (all composite), Evolutions Rail
  • Colors: Match decking collections (Express, Walnut, Coastline, etc.)
  • Infill Options: Composite balusters, aluminum balusters, glass panels
  • Features: Color-matched to decking, pre-routed posts, LED lighting compatible, 25-year warranty
  • Popular For: Matching deck surface, integrated lighting, warm appearance

Trex Railing

  • Cost: $60-105 per linear foot
  • Collections: Transcend (premium), Select (mid-tier), Enhance (entry-level)
  • Colors: Vintage Lantern, Gravel Path, Rope Swing, Classic White, and more
  • Infill Options: Composite balusters, aluminum balusters (Trex Signature), glass (Trex Signature Glass)
  • Features: Hidden fasteners, pre-assembled sections, 25-year warranty, complements Trex decking
  • Popular For: Brand coordination, ease of installation, traditional appearance

Composite Railing Pros/Cons:

Pros:

  • Matches composite decking perfectly
  • No painting or staining required
  • Won't rot, split, or crack
  • Warmer to touch than metal in winter
  • Available in colors that coordinate with deck boards

Cons:

  • Can fade over time (like decking)
  • More expensive than wood, less than aluminum
  • Heavier than aluminum (more difficult to work with)
  • May expand/contract with temperature changes

Railing Code Requirements

  • Height: 36" minimum from deck surface to top of rail (42" for commercial)
  • Baluster Spacing: 4" maximum opening (often called the "sphere test" - a 4" ball cannot pass through)
  • Graspability: Top rail must be graspable (typically 1.25" to 2.675" width)
  • Load Requirements: Rails must withstand 200 lbs concentrated load and 50 lbs per linear foot distributed load
  • Stair Rails: Required for 4 or more risers, 34"-38" height measured from stair nosing

Installation Best Practices

Ledger Board Attachment

  • Remove siding where ledger attaches - never attach through siding
  • Install proper flashing above ledger to prevent water intrusion
  • Use 1/2" x 5" lag screws or through-bolts every 16" (per code requirements)
  • Maintain 1/4" gap between ledger and house for drainage
  • Attach to house rim joist or band board, not just sheathing

Joist Spacing and Sizing

  • Composite/PVC Decking: 16" on-center maximum (12" for some brands)
  • Wood Decking: 16" on-center typical, 24" possible for thicker boards
  • Joist Size: 2x8 for spans up to 10', 2x10 for spans up to 13', 2x12 for longer spans
  • Always verify with manufacturer specifications and local code requirements

Proper Drainage

  • Slope deck away from house: 1/8" per foot minimum
  • Use self-adhesive joist tape to protect tops of joists from moisture
  • Space deck boards with appropriate gaps (1/4" for composite, 1/8" for wood)
  • Install proper flashing at all house connections

Fastener Selection

  • Structural Connections: Hot-dipped galvanized or stainless steel lag screws, through-bolts
  • Joist Hangers: Galvanized or stainless, use manufacturer-specified nails
  • Decking Fasteners: Coated composite screws or hidden fastener systems
  • Never Use: Standard screws or nails with pressure-treated lumber (will corrode)

Common Issues to Avoid

  • Improper Ledger Attachment: Leading cause of deck collapse - must be properly flashed and through-bolted
  • Undersized Footings: Must be below frost line and proper diameter for load
  • Wrong Fasteners: Standard screws corrode rapidly with PT lumber
  • Inadequate Joist Spacing: Composite decking requires closer spacing than wood
  • No Flashing: Water intrusion at ledger board causes rot in house structure
  • Improper Post Bases: Posts embedded in concrete rot at ground line
  • Spacing Violations: Baluster spacing over 4" fails code and inspection

Inspections

Footing Inspection

Required before pouring concrete. Inspector verifies:

  • Hole depth meets frost line requirements
  • Hole diameter adequate for load
  • Proper location per plan
  • Forms installed correctly (if used)

Framing Inspection

Some jurisdictions require inspection before decking installation. Inspector checks:

  • Ledger board properly attached and flashed
  • Joist sizing and spacing per code
  • Proper hardware and fasteners used
  • Blocking installed as required
  • Beam spans within allowable limits

Final Inspection

Conducted after deck completion. Inspector verifies:

  • Railing height meets minimum 36" requirement
  • Baluster spacing 4" maximum
  • Stair rail height 34"-38"
  • All connections secure
  • Work matches approved plan
  • Proper drainage away from house
