Drywall

Drywall covers stocking, hanging, and finishing. This can be a finish material or a base to apply additional surface coverings to after.

Quick Reference

  • Provide the sub with square footage, ceiling height, and stock date
  • Insulation installed and inspected before drywall
  • Glue and screw — screws every 16" on 16 OC framing (2015 MRC 702.3.5)
  • Screw inspection required between hanging and finishing in some jurisdictions
  • Use plastic corner bead and moisture-resistant drywall in bathrooms and wet areas
  • Mark start/stop points on site (e.g., shower areas, tile surrounds)
  • Yard may not carry drywall up or down stairs — confirm with supplier
  • Boom truck can deliver to second floor if access is left open and truck stays 20 ft from power lines

Materials

  • Standard drywall: 4×8 to 4×16 sheets of compressed gypsum with paper face. Also available in 54" wide sheets
    • ½" — standard for most residential walls and ceilings with 16 OC framing
    • ⅝" — required for fire-rated assemblies (Type X); sometimes used on ceilings with 24 OC framing to resist sag but not required
    • ¼" and ⅜" — used for curved walls and layered applications
  • Moisture-resistant drywall — for areas with intermittent moisture exposure (laundry, utility rooms); not rated for direct water contact like shower surrounds
  • Densglass or similar fiberglass-faced sheathing — for high-humidity areas and exterior applications; check local code for where required
  • Higher fire-rating drywall available beyond standard Type X where assemblies require it
  • Drywall adhesive — not the same as construction adhesive (PL, Liquid Nails); use adhesive specifically labeled for drywall
  • Drywall screws — coarse thread for wood framing, fine thread for steel studs

Prerequisites

  • Site clean and swept — drywall crew should not be working around construction debris
  • Plates cut out of door openings
  • Temporary nailers and blocks removed
  • Drywall backing in place at all inside corners, tub/shower flanges, and soffits
  • Pay attention to site access around trusses, corners, and soffits — confirm the sub can physically get sheets to all areas
  • Insulation installed and insulation inspection passed
  • Tub and shower units set (drywall lands on tub/shower flange)
  • Mark windows, doors, and openings that will be drywall wrapped
  • Start/stop points marked on framing (shower areas, tile surrounds, anywhere drywall stops short)

Coordination and Scheduling

  • Lead Time
    • Give the drywall sub approximately one month's notice
    • Provide: address, approximate square footage, wall height, ceiling height, planned stock date
    • Communicate when rough building and insulation will pass
  • Logistics
    • Tell the sub where drywall can be delivered and stocked inside the home
    • Confirm where crew will enter/park
    • Provide access info: door codes, garage codes, lockbox details
  • Site Visit and Takeoff
    • Drywaller does a site visit to measure and determine stocking strategy
    • Leaves with a drywall list: sheet sizes (4×8 through 4×16), thicknesses, any specialty boards (Type X, moisture-resistant)

Stocking and Delivery

  • Drywall is ordered from a drywall yard; yard delivers with a truck, crew, and boom lift
  • The yard will not carry drywall up or down stairs
    • Second floor: stock through a window, door, or other opening using the boom
    • Basement: stock through a walkout door or through an egress window opening
    • Builder may need to leave an egress window opening enlarged for stocking, or open a temporary hole in the second story
  • Builder may need to provide labor to move drywall depending on site conditions
  • Distribute sheets throughout the house — don't stack all material in one room (floor load and workflow considerations)
  • Lean sheets against walls with the finish (white) side facing out so the sub can read markings

Process

  • Hanging
    • Glue drywall to framing and screw off — screws every 16" when glued on 16 OC framing (2015 MRC 702.3.5)
    • Ceilings first, then walls — hang perpendicular to framing where possible to span more members
    • Stagger joints — no four-corner intersections; offset seams from adjacent sheets
    • Drive screws slightly below paper surface without breaking paper face
    • Cut out for electrical boxes, plumbing access panels, and HVAC registers as you go
    • Leave ⅛" gap at floor — drywall should not sit directly on subfloor/concrete
  • Screw Inspection
    • Screw inspection happens after hanging and before finishing begins
    • Inspector checks screw spacing, screw depth (dimpled but not broken through paper), edge distances, and that all seams land on framing
  • Finishing (Tape and Mud)
    • First coat (tape coat) — embed paper or mesh tape in joint compound over all seams, corners, and screw heads
    • Second coat (fill/block coat) — wider pass of compound to fill and smooth seams
    • Third coat (skim/finish coat) — feathered thin to blend seams invisible
    • Sand between coats as needed; final sand after last coat
    • Inside corners: paper tape folded and set in compound
    • Outside corners: metal or vinyl corner bead, set and mudded
  • Finish Levels
    • Level 0: No taping or finishing (used above ceilings in non-accessible areas)
    • Level 1: Tape embedded in compound, no additional coats (fire-rated areas, above ceilings, attics, garages)
    • Level 2: Tape + one coat compound (where tile or paneling covers the surface)
    • Level 3: Tape + two coats (for heavy textured finishes)
    • Level 4: Tape + three coats (standard for paint with flat or light texture — most residential)
    • Level 5: Level 4 + skim coat over entire surface (for critical lighting or gloss/semi-gloss paint where imperfections show)

Sequencing After Drywall

  • Drywall must be up before priming can begin
  • Prime right after drywall finishing — seals in dust and keeps the site cleaner
  • Priming before stained trim is especially important — drywall dust can get into wood grain and will not come out
  • Typical sequence: hang → screw inspection → finish (tape/mud/sand) → prime → paint → trim/doors → flooring

Inspections

  • Screw Inspection: Required between hanging and finishing — inspector checks screw spacing, depth, and that seams land on framing
  • Fire-Rated Assemblies: Inspector may verify correct board type (Type X), layer count, and fastener pattern in fire-rated walls and ceilings

Best Practices

  • Walk the site before stocking day to confirm all prerequisites are met — missing backing or failed inspections will delay the entire drywall sequence
  • Confirm boom truck access before stocking is scheduled — overhead wires, tight driveways, and tree limbs can prevent delivery
  • Photograph all rough-in wiring, plumbing, and ductwork before drywall covers it — this is the last chance to document what's in the walls
  • Keep the site heated to at least 50°F during finishing — joint compound will not cure properly in cold conditions
  • Provide ventilation during finishing to help compound dry and reduce humidity
  • Don't rush the finish coats — drying time between coats prevents cracking, bubbling, and poor adhesion
  • Mark start/stop points clearly on framing so hangers know where to leave gaps for tile, stone, or other finishes
  • Use screws, not nails — nails are code-allowed but screws hold better and are less likely to pop over time

Client Communication

  • Drywall is one of the dustiest phases — advise clients to seal off living areas if occupied, and expect dust migration even with containment
  • Explain the multi-day finishing process: three coats of mud with drying time between each means finishing takes longer than hanging
  • Prime immediately after finishing to seal dust before trim work begins — especially critical before stained trim
  • Set expectations about visible seams: some joint visibility under raking light is normal even with Level 4 finish; Level 5 is available where critical lighting conditions exist
  • Seasonal movement: explain that hairline cracks at corners and seams can appear in the first year as the framing dries out and the house settles — this is normal and addressed at the one-year touch-up
