Mold Remediation

Small-scale mold cleanup is a routine part of remodeling. Pull back drywall on a window that's been leaking for a few years and you'll find it. This page covers how to assess the situation, set up safely, and treat affected framing and drywall with validated products. For large infestations or hidden HVAC contamination, call a certified remediator.

Quick Reference

  • EPA guideline: areas under 10 sq ft can generally be handled by a trained contractor; 10–100 sq ft requires contractor-level precautions; over 100 sq ft or any HVAC contamination requires a certified remediator
  • Fix the moisture source before treating mold. Treating mold on wet material is pointless
  • Kill first, then remove staining if needed, then encapsulate. These are separate steps and separate products
  • RMR-86 and Zinsser Mold Killing Primer are not EPA-registered mold killers. Use them in the right step or not at all
  • Concrobium in a pump sprayer is the simplest single-product DIY approach. No bleach, no rinsing required
  • For pro-grade work: RMR-141 or Vital Oxide to kill, RMR-86 if staining is severe, Foster 40-25 to encapsulate
  • Do not treat and cover mold in occupied space without containment. Disturbance releases spores

Scope: DIY vs. Call a Pro

  • Under 10 sq ft (EPA guideline): Trained contractor can handle with proper PPE and containment. Typical finding: one wall cavity behind a leaking window or small roof leak
  • 10–100 sq ft: Contractor-level work. Full containment, negative air pressure recommended, and full PPE required. Notify the homeowner in writing before starting
  • Over 100 sq ft, or mold in HVAC ductwork, or visible mold in more than two rooms: Call a certified remediator (IICRC S520 standard). Do not disturb until they assess
  • Black mold (Stachybotrys): Often overstated in severity by the media, but still warrants pro assessment if coverage is widespread. A surface test kit won't tell you species — only lab culture can
  • When in doubt on scope, document with photos, stop work, and get a remediator assessment before proceeding

PPE and Containment

  • PPE
    • Minimum for small cleanup: N95 respirator, nitrile gloves, safety glasses
    • For anything over 10 sq ft or heavy growth: half-face respirator with P100 filters, disposable Tyvek suit, nitrile gloves taped at the cuffs
    • Do not use a dust mask. Mold spores pass through them
  • Containment
    • Seal HVAC registers and returns in the work area before starting; tape 6-mil poly over them
    • For larger areas: build a poly barrier around the work zone with a zipper entry. Run a negative air machine exhausting outside (not into another room or attic) to keep spore count low in adjacent spaces
    • Bag demo material in 6-mil poly bags and seal before moving through the house

Process

  • Step 1: Find and Fix the Source
    • Identify where the water came from: failed window flashing, missing caulk, roof leak, plumbing leak, condensation
    • Repair the source before doing anything else. Treating mold on material that is still wet or will get wet again is a waste of product and time
    • Confirm the assembly is dry before treating. Wood framing at or above 19% moisture content (MC) will continue to support mold growth even after treatment. Use a pin-type moisture meter to verify the framing has dried below 16% MC before applying any biocide
  • Step 2: Demo Affected Material
    • Remove drywall and insulation with visible or suspected mold growth. These are not worth treating in place; demo and replace
    • For framing with surface mold only, treatment in place is acceptable if the wood is structurally sound and fully dry
    • Bag demo debris in 6-mil poly bags and seal before moving through occupied spaces
    • HEPA-vacuum surfaces after demo to remove loose spore load before applying any liquid product
  • Step 3: Kill
    • Apply an EPA-registered biocide to all affected framing and sheathing surfaces
    • See Products section below for validated options by availability and use case
    • Follow product dwell times before scrubbing or wiping (Concrobium: let dry fully; Foster 40-80: 10-minute dwell; Vital Oxide: no rinse required)
    • For heavy surface growth, scrub with a stiff brush after the dwell time, then HEPA-vac, then apply a second coat
  • Step 4: Remove Staining (if needed)
    • Staining on drywall paper or wood grain after killing is cosmetic but may need to be addressed before priming
    • RMR-86 (sodium hypochlorite) is the standard stain remover for this step. It works in 15–60 seconds on surface stains but is not a mold killer on its own
    • Use with heavy ventilation and full PPE; wear splash goggles
    • Do not use RMR-86 on materials you haven't already killed with a registered biocide
  • Step 5: Dry
    • After wet treatment products, allow framing to fully dry before encapsulating. Sealing wet or damp wood traps moisture and promotes regrowth. Use fans and dehumidifiers and verify MC is below 16% before proceeding
  • Step 6: Encapsulate
    • Apply an antimicrobial encapsulant coating to treated framing and sheathing before closing the wall
    • This creates a long-term barrier that inhibits future growth if minor moisture recurs
    • Foster 40-25 Full Defense is the correct product for porous surfaces (framing, sheathing, OSB). Covers 300 sq ft per gallon
    • Zinsser Mold Killing Primer is labeled for non-porous surfaces only and is not approved as a professional encapsulant for exposed framing or drywall in humid conditions. Use it as a primer on finished painted drywall you are keeping, not on raw wood
  • Step 7: Rebuild
    • Once encapsulant is cured, reinstall insulation, drywall, and any trim
    • Photograph completed remediation before closing the wall for documentation
    • Replace window flashing, caulk, or weatherstripping as part of the rebuild — not as a separate punch item

Products

Kill-First (Biocides / Disinfectants)

  • Concrobium Mold Control — Bleach-free, non-toxic biocide. Active chemistry (sodium carbonate/trisodium phosphate) crushes mold cells as it dries and leaves an antimicrobial barrier. Apply by pump sprayer, let dry fully, then scrub or HEPA-vac. Best single-product DIY option. Available in 1-gallon jugs at Lowe's
  • Vital Oxide (EPA Reg. #82972-1, Category IV — lowest toxicity) — Hospital disinfectant used by professional remediation companies. Active ingredient is chlorine dioxide; penetrates and eliminates mold on porous and non-porous surfaces including drywall, wood framing, concrete, and carpet. No rinse required, no VOCs, non-corrosive. Demonstrated residual effect up to seven months per independent study. Order online or through restoration suppliers; not at Lowe's or Menards
  • Benefect Botanical Decon 30 — EPA-registered botanical disinfectant (thyme oil based) used in water damage restoration. Kills 99.99%+ of bacteria and fungi in 30 seconds on porous and semi-porous surfaces without bleach or synthetic chemicals. This is a second-step disinfectant after physical removal, not a stain remover. Available through restoration supply houses
  • Foster 40-80 First Defense (EPA Reg. #6836-152) — Hospital-grade EPA-registered fungicide, cleaner, disinfectant, and deodorizer in one. Designed specifically for water damage restoration on porous, semi-porous, and non-porous surfaces including drywall, subfloors, framing, and trim. Requires 10-minute dwell time. Available through insulation and abatement suppliers
  • RMR-141 — EPA-registered quat-ammonium disinfectant. Kills mold, bacteria, and viruses on porous surfaces including drywall and wood; creates a protective barrier against regrowth. This is the "kill" step in the RMR two-step system. Available online and on Amazon

Stain Removal (After Kill Step Only)

  • RMR-86 — Sodium hypochlorite (bleach-based) stain eliminator. Not an EPA-registered mold killer. Use it after a biocide has already killed the mold, to remove the dark staining left in drywall paper or wood grain. Works in 15–60 seconds on stains. Use with heavy ventilation and full PPE. Available online and on Amazon

Encapsulation / Final Coat

  • Foster 40-25 Full Defense Fungicidal Protective Coating — EPA-registered antimicrobial encapsulant for porous surfaces after remediation. Breathable, covers 300 sq ft per gallon, designed to prevent long-term mold regrowth. This is what remediation contractors use where Zinsser would fail on exposed framing or drywall in humid spaces. Available through abatement suppliers
  • Zinsser Mold Killing Primer (EPA Reg. #87469-1) — Antimicrobial primer that kills residual surface mold and seals before topcoat. Labeled for non-porous surfaces only; not approved for use as a professional encapsulant on exposed porous wood or drywall in humid conditions. Use it as a primer coat on finished painted drywall you are keeping, followed by a stain-blocking primer like Zinsser BIN if needed

Best Practices

  • Kill and encapsulate are separate steps that require separate products. Priming over live mold, or using a stain remover as a biocide, will not stop regrowth
  • The moisture source is the actual problem. Mold is a symptom. Every remediation starts with finding and fixing the leak or condensation issue
  • If you open a wall and the mold is only on the drywall paper, not the framing, the exposure was likely short-lived. If framing is heavily colonized or structurally compromised, extend the scope
  • HEPA-vac before you spray. Applying a liquid biocide to a heavy spore load without removing loose material first just moves the problem
  • Document everything with photos: the discovery, the demo, the framing condition, the treatment, and the encapsulant coat before closing the wall. This protects you if a callback comes in six months
  • Never cover mold with new drywall without treating the framing. The mold is still viable behind the paper and will continue growing
  • Concrobium is the safest product for an occupied home with no relocation. No bleach, no VOCs, no mandatory ventilation. For unoccupied spaces or vacant demo sites, stronger chemistry (Vital Oxide, Foster 40-80) is appropriate

Client Communication

  • Notify the client before opening any wall where mold is suspected. Verbal is fine but follow up in writing
  • Explain that mold discovery during demo can extend the scope and timeline. Most clients accept this if they understand why the source has to be fixed and why new material can't go in wet or contaminated framing
  • Clarify what you are doing (surface mold removal on framing) vs. what requires a certified remediator (large-scale infestation, HVAC contamination). Clients often conflate the two after seeing news coverage of black mold
  • After remediation, confirm in writing that affected material was removed or treated, the moisture source was repaired, and the encapsulant was applied before closing the wall
