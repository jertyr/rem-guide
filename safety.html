Jobsite Safety

This page is a no-nonsense reference for what keeps a crew safe and keeps OSHA from writing you up. The goal is two things at once: have the equipment, training, and paperwork that an inspector looks for, and actually use them in a practical way. Most jobsite citations come down to a short list of items that should be on every truck and a few documents that should exist before the first day. The citations below point to the federal OSHA construction standard (29 CFR 1926) so anyone using this page can trace a requirement back to the rule and speak to it on site. State plans and local rules can be stricter, so treat these as the floor, not the ceiling.

Quick Reference

  • Every van carries the PPE and equipment checklist below; a printable copy lives in each truck
  • OSHA can cite the controlling employer for hazards it creates or controls, so the gear and paperwork need to be real, not just on a list
  • A written safety plan, posted emergency contacts, and accessible SDS sheets should exist before work starts
  • Someone on site is the designated competent person who can spot hazards and has authority to fix them (1926.20(b)(2), 1926.32(f))
  • PPE is required when a hazard is present; some items (like hard hats) are exposure based, not always-on

Required PPE and Safety Equipment

The list below is the on-site equipment an inspector is most likely to ask about. Each item links to the OSHA section that governs it, because the rule often carries specifics about the equipment itself. A printable version for the van is the standalone PPE and Safety Equipment Checklist.

Personal Protective Equipment

  • Z87 safety glasses (1926.102) — Eye and face protection is required wherever there is a hazard from flying particles, dust, or chemical splash. Lenses must meet ANSI Z87.1, marked Z87 (or Z87+ for high impact) on the frame or lens. Keep a box of clear and tinted on hand.
  • Hard hat (1926.100) — Required where there is a possible danger of head injury from impact, falling or flying objects, or electrical shock. Must meet ANSI Z89.1. This is exposure based, not always required; see When Is a Hard Hat Required? below.
  • Hearing protection (1926.101, 1926.52) — Required when noise exposure meets or exceeds a 90 dBA 8-hour average; protection must be made available at 85 dBA. Plugs or muffs for anyone running saws, nailers, hammer drills, or breakers.
  • N95 masks (1910.134) — Filtering facepiece for nuisance and general dust. N95 filters 95% of non-oil particles. Voluntary use still requires giving workers Appendix D of 1910.134.
  • P100 respirator (1910.134, 1926.1153) — Half-mask with P100 cartridges (99.97%, oil-proof) for silica, lead, mold, and heavy dust. Required use triggers a written program, medical evaluation, and annual fit testing. See Training below.
  • Work gloves (1926.95) — General PPE selected for the hazard: cut-resistant for handling sheet goods and blades, chemical gloves for solvents and finishes.
  • Foot protection (1926.96) — Protective footwear meeting ASTM F2413 where there is a risk of foot injury from falling or rolling objects or puncture. Standard expectation for demo and framing.
  • High-visibility vest (1926.201, MUTCD) — Situational. Required for flaggers and anyone exposed to public traffic or moving equipment. Keep a couple in the van for road-adjacent work.

Site Equipment

  • First aid kit (1926.50) — First aid supplies must be readily available. The kit should be a weatherproof container with individual sealed packages, checked weekly and after use, and stocked for the size of the crew. ANSI/ISEA Z308.1 is the consensus standard for contents.
  • Fire extinguisher (1926.150(c)) — At least one rated not less than 2A for every 3,000 sq. ft. of the protected building area, with travel distance to the nearest unit not exceeding 100 feet. A 10B unit is required within 50 feet wherever more than 5 gallons of flammable or combustible liquid or 5 lbs of flammable gas is in use.
  • GFCI-protected cords (1926.404(b)(1)) — All 120V, 15- and 20-amp receptacles not part of the permanent wiring and in use by workers must be GFCI protected, or the employer runs an assured equipment grounding conductor program. In practice: GFCI on the cord or a GFCI-protected temporary panel for every tool.
  • Posted emergency contacts (1926.50(f)) — Telephone numbers of physicians, hospitals, and ambulances must be conspicuously posted where 911 is not the sole means of contact. Keep the nearest hospital and address on the posting.
  • Electrical pen tester (non-contact voltage tester; supports 1926.416, 1926.417) — No specific PPE rule, but workers may not work near energized parts until they are de-energized and verified. A non-contact tester is the practical tool for confirming a circuit is dead before opening a box or cutting into a wall.

Documentation On Site

These belong on the checklist as a reminder to make electronic copies available, even though the paper does not have to live in the van.

  • Safety Data Sheets (SDS, formerly MSDS) (1910.1200, adopted for construction by 1926.59) — An SDS must be readily accessible to workers for every hazardous chemical on site (adhesives, solvents, fuels, finishes). Electronic access is allowed as long as workers can reach it without barriers and there is a backup if the system is down.
  • Written safety plan / accident prevention program (1926.20(b)(1)) — The employer must initiate and maintain programs to provide for safe working conditions. See the section below.
  • Emergency action plan (1926.35) — Required where other standards call for one; covers evacuation routes, reporting, and accounting for workers.

When Is a Hard Hat Required?

Hard hats trip people up because they are not always required. The rule is exposure based. OSHA 1926.100 requires head protection where there is a possible danger of head injury from impact, from falling or flying objects, or from electrical shock and burns. When no one is working overhead and there is no exposure to head injury, OSHA does not require a hard hat. This is why a roofer alone on a roof deck with no overhead work is not automatically in violation, and it is also why the moment another trade is working above, head protection is back in play.

  • Required: anyone below overhead work, near crane or lift operations, in excavations with loads passing overhead, around exposed energized conductors, or in low-clearance framing
  • Helmets must meet ANSI Z89.1; Type I protects against top impact, Type II adds lateral impact protection
  • Class designation matters near electrical: Class E (electrical, 20kV), Class G (general, 2.2kV), Class C (conductive, no electrical protection)
  • OSHA sets the floor; an employer can require hard hats site-wide at all times, which is the simplest way to stay clear of the judgment call

Field rule of thumb: if anything can fall on a head, or work is happening overhead, hard hats are on. A blanket site policy removes the argument with an inspector.

Written Safety Plan and Emergency Contacts

A written safety plan is the document that ties the rest together. OSHA 1926.20(b) requires the employer to initiate and maintain accident prevention programs and to designate competent persons to run them. The plan does not need to be long, but it should be specific to how the company actually works.

  • Company safety policy and the name of the designated safety coordinator / competent person (1926.20(b)(2), 1926.32(f))
  • Hazard communication program and where SDS sheets are accessed (1910.1200)
  • Required PPE by task and who provides it
  • Emergency action plan: nearest hospital, evacuation, reporting an injury, severe weather (1926.35)
  • Posted emergency contacts at each active site (1926.50(f))
  • Trade-specific programs that apply to your work: silica exposure control plan (1926.1153(g)), fall protection, respiratory protection (1910.134)

Site Safety Walks and Inspections

OSHA 1926.20(b)(2) requires frequent and regular inspections of the jobsite, materials, and equipment by competent persons. This is the requirement behind a routine safety walk. Documenting the walk on a short form shows the program is real and gives you a record of what was found and fixed. A printable form is the standalone Site Safety Inspection Form.

  • Walk the site on a regular cadence, not just when something goes wrong
  • The person walking it should be the competent person: able to identify hazards and authorized to correct them on the spot (1926.32(f))
  • Note the hazard, the corrective action, and the date closed
  • Keep the completed forms; they are your evidence of an active program

Training and Certifications

Equipment without training is still a citation. Keep records of who is trained and when. The items below are the certifications most relevant to remodeling work.

  • First aid / CPR (1926.50(c)) — Where a medical facility is not in near proximity, a person trained to render first aid must be available on site, with a valid certificate from a recognized provider (American Red Cross, American Heart Association, or equivalent). Certificates typically renew every two years.
  • Respirator fit test and medical evaluation (1910.134(e), (f)) — Before required respirator use, a worker must pass a medical evaluation, then a fit test for the specific make, model, and size. Fit testing is repeated at least annually and whenever facial changes could affect the seal. Tight-fitting respirators require a clean-shaven seal area.
  • Silica awareness training (1926.1153(i), 1910.1200) — Anyone cutting, grinding, or drilling concrete, masonry, tile, or stone must be trained on silica hazards, the tasks that create exposure, the controls in the company exposure control plan, and the contents of Table 1 (water and vacuum dust controls). Pair with the written exposure control plan and a designated competent person.
  • Ladder safety training (1926.1060) — A competent person must train each worker who uses ladders on hazard recognition, correct setup and use, load limits, and the 4:1 setup ratio (1 foot out for every 4 feet of working height) and the 3-foot extension above the landing. Retraining is required when unsafe use is observed.
  • Safety coordinator / competent person (1926.20(b), 1926.32(f)) — The designated person who runs the safety program, conducts the inspections, and has authority to stop work and correct hazards. Many trade-specific standards (silica, excavation, scaffolding, fall protection) each require a competent person, so this role and its training should be defined in the safety plan.
