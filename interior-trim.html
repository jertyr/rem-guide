Interior Trim

Interior trim is the finish carpentry that closes out a remodel: doors, casing, base, and the details around windows and openings. It is the most visible work on the job and it gets looked at from a foot away, so it depends on a dry building, a flat substrate, and material that has stopped moving before it goes on the wall.

Quick Reference

  • Order doors and trim early. Stain grade and anything non-stock runs long
  • Confirm size, hand, and swing on every opening before the order goes in
  • Building conditioned and dry before trim is delivered, not after
  • Let material acclimate inside the conditioned space before it gets installed
  • Trim goes in after finish flooring so base and casing land on the finished floor
  • Trim installs between the two painting phases: primed walls before, finish coats after
  • Paint grade or stain grade is an ordering decision, not a field decision
  • Check openings plumb, square, and correctly sized before doors show up
  • The garage to house door has code requirements that a standard interior door will not meet (IRC R302.5.1)

Prerequisites

  • Drywall finished, sanded, and primed. See Drywall
  • Finish flooring installed. Base and casing are cut to the finished floor, and door jambs need the final floor height to set the undercut. See Flooring and Tile
  • Building conditioned and dry, with heat or air running and holding
  • Trim delivered and stickered flat inside the conditioned space to acclimate, typically several days ahead of install
  • Openings framed plumb, square, and to the right rough opening
  • Registers, thermostats, outlets, and switches located clear of casing and base runs
  • Door schedule confirmed: location, size, hand, swing, and type for every opening
  • Hardware on site, or at least selected, so bore and backset are known

Materials and Components

  • Doors
    • Prehung: door, jamb, and stop assembled and hung as a unit. Standard for new openings
    • Slab: door only, hung in an existing jamb. Requires mortising hinges and boring for hardware
    • Bifold and bypass for closets
    • Pocket doors, which need the frame set at framing, not at trim
    • Barn door hardware, which needs blocking and wall clearance planned early
  • Door specifications to confirm at order
    • Thickness: 1 3/8" is standard for interior doors; 1 3/4" for heavier or taller units
    • Height: 6'8" standard, with 7'0" and 8'0" common upgrades
    • Width: 2'0" through 3'0", normally in 2" increments
    • Core: hollow core, solid core, or solid wood. Solid core is the usual upgrade for sound and feel
    • Paint grade (primed MDF or finger joint) or stain grade (specify species)
    • Jamb width: 4 9/16" for a 2×4 wall and 6 9/16" for a 2×6 wall, both assuming 1/2" drywall. Measure the actual wall on a remodel instead of assuming
  • Trim
    • Casing for doors and windows
    • Base, with shoe or quarter round where the floor calls for it
    • Window stool and apron
    • Crown, chair rail, wainscot, and paneling where specified
    • Material: primed finger joint, MDF, poplar, or a hardwood matched to the stain
  • Fasteners and consumables
    • 15 or 16 gauge finish nails for jambs, casing, and base; 18 gauge brads for shoe, returns, and delicate pieces
    • Shims, wood glue, construction adhesive
    • Caulk, wood filler, and putty matched to the finish

Process

  • Verify the openings
    • Check every rough opening for size, plumb, and square before unwrapping doors
    • A prehung rough opening is normally the door width plus 2" and the height plus 2" to 2 1/2"
    • Check the floor across the opening for level. An out of level floor changes how the jamb gets set
    • Confirm hand and swing against the plan at the opening, before the unit is cut in
  • Hang the doors
    • Set and shim the hinge jamb first, plumb in both directions, and fasten through shims behind each hinge
    • Set the strike jamb to a consistent reveal around the door, then shim and fasten at the strike
    • Check the door swings and stays where it is put, then adjust before nailing off
    • Run at least one fastener per hinge into the framing, not just the jamb, on heavier doors
    • Slab doors: mortise hinges to match the existing jamb, then bore for hardware. Standard bore is 2 1/8" face and 1" edge, with a 2 3/8" or 2 3/4" backset
  • Case the openings
    • Set a consistent reveal on the jamb edge, commonly 3/16", and mark or gauge it rather than eyeballing each leg
    • Glue and pin miters, then fasten the casing to the jamb and to the framing
    • Window stool first, then the apron, then the side and head casing
  • Base and specialty trim
    • Cope inside corners; miter and glue outside corners
    • Scarf long runs over a stud and glue the joint
    • Add shoe or quarter round where the flooring needs the expansion gap covered
    • Crown, wainscot, and paneling last, after base and casing are set
  • Hardware and punch out
    • Install passage, privacy, and dummy sets per the schedule
    • Set stops so hardware does not hit the wall
    • Fill nail holes, caulk to the wall and at joints, and sand as the finish requires
    • Walk each opening: reveal consistent, door latches, no rubbing, and the swing is right

Best Practices

  • Build the door schedule off the plan and walk it in the field before ordering. Hand and swing errors are expensive and slow to fix once the units are on site
  • Label every door and jamb by opening as it is delivered, and keep the label on until it is hung
  • Keep stain grade material off the floor, out of the traffic path, and away from drywall dust. Dust in the grain shows through the stain
  • Prime or seal the back of trim that will sit against a cold or damp wall
  • Gauge the reveal instead of eyeballing it. A consistent reveal is what makes trim read as clean work
  • Cope inside corners so the joint stays tight when the material moves
  • Glue every miter. Caulk hides a gap for a season; glue holds the joint
  • Nail into framing wherever a piece will get pushed or leaned on, especially base and door stops
  • Set the last piece of trim only after the flooring is protected. Trim work drops tools and offcuts
  • Cut a sample of each profile and hold it in the room before running the whole house

Inspections

  • Interior trim on its own is generally not inspected. What gets checked at final is the code driven hardware and openings around it
  • Garage to dwelling door: solid wood not less than 1 3/8" thick, solid or honeycomb core steel not less than 1 3/8" thick, or a 20 minute fire rated door (IRC R302.5.1). Current IRC editions also require the door to be self latching and equipped with a self closing or automatic closing device; confirm against the edition your jurisdiction has adopted, because this requirement has changed between code cycles
  • Openings from a garage directly into a room used for sleeping are not permitted (IRC R302.5.1)
  • Handrails, guards, and stair trim are inspected as part of the stair assembly. See Stairs

Client Communication

  • Trim is the work clients inspect the closest. Expect a punch walk and budget the time for it
  • Stain grade costs more and takes longer than paint grade, in both material and labor. Settle that at selection, not at install
  • Selections drive the schedule: door style, trim profile, and hardware all need to be locked before anything can be ordered
  • Filler and caulk are normal parts of a paint grade finish. Stain grade hides much less, which is part of why it costs more
  • Doors may be pulled and finished off site by the painter, which leaves openings bare for a few days. See Painting
  • Wood moves with the seasons. Small seasonal gaps at joints are expected and are not a defect
