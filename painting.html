Painting

Interior painting on a remodel. Painting is almost always subcontracted, so the work that falls to the GC is scope, sequence, and protection. The painter does what the contract says, not what you assumed. Most painting disputes trace back to a surface nobody wrote down.

Quick Reference

  • If it is not written in the scope, it is not included
  • Windows are not in the painting scope unless explicitly specified
  • Scope room by room and surface by surface: ceiling, walls, trim, doors, jambs
  • Specify coat count and sheen for every surface, not just color
  • Two phases: prime before finish flooring, finish coats after trim is installed
  • Flooring goes in between the two phases; fixtures go in after the final coat
  • Dark colors often need a third coat. Price it and schedule it when the color is selected
  • Colors approved on the actual wall, in that room's lighting, before the painter is scheduled
  • Touch-up is the last punch item, after every other trade is finished

Scope: What to Specify

Write the scope surface by surface, room by room, and hand it to the painter at bid rather than during the job. The Painting Worksheet is built for this.

  • Surfaces to list explicitly
    • Walls, per room
    • Ceilings, per room. Often priced separately from walls, so silence reads as excluded
    • Interior trim per room: base, casing, crown
    • Interior doors: face, back, and edge. State which faces are included
    • Door frames and jambs
    • Window sashes, frames, and sills. Include them or exclude them, but do not leave it open
    • Built-ins and shelving
    • Cabinets and furniture, inside and out. Cabinet interiors are their own line
    • Exterior doors, usually a separate line. See Exterior Doors
    • Exterior trim, if any is in the contract
  • For every surface, specify
    • Number of coats
    • Sheen. Flat ceilings, eggshell walls, semi-gloss trim is a common baseline, not a rule
    • Color and brand or product line
    • Stain grade or paint grade, where trim is involved
  • Set the acceptance standard in the contract so "done" is defined before anyone argues about it. PCA Standard P1 defines a properly painted surface as uniform in appearance, color, texture, hiding, and sheen, free of lumps, skins, runs, sags, holidays, misses, and insufficient coverage, judged without magnification from 39 inches (1 meter) or more, under finished lighting, from a normal viewing position

Prerequisites

  • Drywall hung, finished to the specified level, and repaired. See Drywall
  • Finish level matched to the sheen and the lighting. GA-214 Level 4 is the standard for flat paint; Level 5 is called for under gloss or semi-gloss and under critical lighting such as large windows, skylights, or wall sconces that rake the surface
  • The GC, not the painter, accepts the substrate as ready to finish. PCA Standard P4 puts that call on the contracting entity; the painter is only obligated to check that the finish will adhere and perform
  • Colors and sheens approved in writing, room by room
  • Building conditioned and dry. Most interior latex products carry a 50°F minimum application temperature; check the data sheet for the product in use
  • Floor protection down and countertops, fixtures, and finished surfaces masked
  • Furniture and stored material moved out of the work area

Process

  • Phase 1: Prime, before finish flooring
    • Painter primes sanded drywall. This seals drywall dust and locks the surface down
    • Critical ahead of stain grade trim. Drywall dust in the wood grain ruins the stain finish
    • PVA drywall primer is a basecoat, not a finish; it has to be topcoated with latex or alkyd to perform
    • This visit happens before flooring goes in
  • Between phases
    • Finish flooring installed. See Flooring and Tile
    • Interior trim installed
    • Countertops templated and set. Protect them before the painter comes back. See Countertops
  • Phase 2: Finish coats, after trim is installed
    • Spray trim; cut in and roll walls and ceilings
    • Two coats on walls is standard production. Dark colors often take a third
    • Painters spraying trim plan on one trim color per job. Swapping colors mid-job is disruptive and is not expected unless it was specified
  • Stain grade trim
    • Separate workflow from painted trim. Prime the wood first to seal it, then stain
    • Full sequence: sand, water pop, condition, stain, three topcoats
    • Conditioner matters most on softwoods and maple, which take stain unevenly. Oil-based conditioner wants stain applied within about two hours, so plan the day around it
  • Doors sent off site
    • Interior doors are commonly pulled and finished at the painter's shop, then returned. This is normal
    • Plan on 2 to 4 days off site. The building needs to be secure and weather-tight with the openings bare
    • Track each door by location so it comes back to the right opening
  • After Phase 2
    • Fixtures, lights, switches, outlets, and hardware go in after the final coat
    • Final touch-up after every other trade is done

Protection and Prep

  • GC provides jobsite protection before the painter starts: Ram Board or rosin paper over finished floors, masking on installed countertops, cabinets, and fixtures
  • Painter provides their own tape and drop cloths while working
  • Moving furniture and stored material out of the work area is a GC coordination task, not the painter's job unless the contract says so
  • Write who provides what into the contract. This is a small line item that prevents a real argument
  • See Jobsite Protection

Best Practices

  • Hand the painter a room-by-room scope matrix at bid. Every surface, with coat count, sheen, and color. Bids priced off the same sheet are comparable, and "that was not in my bid" stops being a conversation
  • Ceilings, trim, and second coats are the three items subs most often price out of a bid. Call them out by name in the scope
  • Lock colors before the painter is scheduled. The painter cannot order material or hold a slot without them, so a late color pushes the paint phase and everything stacked behind it
  • Flag dark colors when they are selected, not when the painter is on site. A third coat is added cost and added schedule
  • Build in at least one site visit per phase. The painting phase is usually the busiest stretch of the job and painters can work several days without a GC on site
  • At each check-in, verify coverage on the first coat before the second goes on, verify sheens against the spec, and hold trim and cabinet colors against the approved sample
  • Walk the first room before the painter rolls the rest of the house. Sheen and color read differently on a wall than on a chip
  • Hold fixture installs until paint is done. If the schedule forces an overlap, protect the finished fixtures and tell the painter what is already in
  • Leave labeled leftover paint with the owner, marked by room and surface, and record the product line and formula on the worksheet. Touch-up a year out is only as good as the record

Sign-Off and Touch-Up

  • Touch-up is a punch list item on every project. Plan on the painter coming back and put it in the contract, or at minimum set the expectation up front
  • Do not sign off on painting until every other trade is finished. Flooring, fixtures, and hardware installs all leave scuffs and dings
  • Separate touch-up from damage caused by others when you write the contract. PCA Standard P1 treats correcting the painter's own workmanship and repairing damage done by other trades as two different obligations, with different parties paying
  • Walk the job with the painter at final rather than sending a list. Sheen and coverage arguments settle faster standing in the room

Client Communication

  • Windows are the most common surprise. Clients assume window sashes, frames, and sills are included. Say so at the bid stage, in writing, either way
  • Color selection is a client deliverable with a deadline, not a nice-to-have. Put the date in the schedule ahead of the painter's first visit
  • Approve colors with samples on the actual wall, in that room's lighting, at different times of day. Digital swatches and paper chips do not hold up
  • Cabinet and trim colors get confirmed against the approved sample before anything is sprayed. A wrong cabinet color surfaces at installation, which is the worst time and the most expensive fix
  • Explain the two-phase sequence up front. Clients see primed walls and assume painting is finished, then wonder why the painter left
  • Dark colors may need a third coat. That is added cost and added days, and it is easier to discuss at selection than at invoicing
  • Interior doors may leave the site for a few days. Say when, and say which openings will be bare
  • Set the touch-up expectation early. Fresh paint gets marked during finish installs, and the last touch-up comes after everything else is done
