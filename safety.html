Jobsite Safety

What belongs on every jobsite to keep a crew safe and meet OSHA requirements. Citations point to the federal construction standard (29 CFR 1926) so you can trace any item back to the rule; state and local rules can be stricter.

Required PPE and Safety Equipment

The PPE and Safety Equipment Checklist is the master list of what belongs in every van. Print it and it doubles as the van check and the restock order. What follows is when the rules require each item:

  • Eyes (1926.102): Z87 glasses whenever the eyes are exposed to flying particles, dust, or chemical splash; on a remodel that means mudding a ceiling, cutting, nailing, drilling, and grinding.
  • Head (1926.100): hard hat where head injury from falling or flying objects or electrical shock is possible. Exposure based; see When Is a Hard Hat Required?
  • Hearing (1926.101, 1926.52): required at a 90 dBA 8-hour average, made available at 85 dBA; wear it running saws, routers, hammer drills, and nailers.
  • Lungs (1910.134): N95 for nuisance dust (voluntary use still requires Appendix D); P100 for silica, lead, mold, mixing concrete or mud, and all dry cutting of masonry. Required use triggers a written program, medical evaluation, and annual fit test.
  • Hands (1926.95): cut-resistant gloves around blades and sheet goods, chemical gloves for solvents and finishes.
  • Feet (1926.96): ASTM F2413 footwear where falling or rolling material or nails underfoot are a danger; heavy material handling and demo.
  • High visibility (1926.201): exposed to public traffic or working around heavy equipment.
  • Site equipment: first aid kit stocked to crew size (1926.50); fire extinguisher 2A per 3,000 sq. ft. within 100 feet, a 10B within 50 feet of more than 5 gallons of flammables (1926.150(c)); GFCI on all temporary 120V receptacles (1926.404(b)(1)); emergency contacts posted (1926.50(f)); verify circuits dead before opening a wall (1926.416).
  • Documentation: SDS accessible for every chemical on site (1910.1200); written safety plan (1926.20(b)(1)); emergency action plan (1926.35).

When Is a Hard Hat Required?

Head protection is required where there is a possible danger of head injury from impact, falling or flying objects, or electrical shock (1926.100). No overhead work and no exposure means none is required; the moment another trade works above, it is back in play.

  • Required below overhead work, near crane or lift operations, in excavations with loads passing overhead, around exposed energized conductors, and in low-clearance framing
  • ANSI Z89.1: Type I for top impact, Type II adds lateral impact
  • Class E (electrical, 20kV), Class G (general, 2.2kV), Class C (conductive, no electrical protection)

A blanket site policy is the simplest way to meet the requirement across the whole site.

Written Safety Plan and Emergency Contacts

OSHA 1926.20(b) requires the employer to maintain an accident prevention program and designate competent persons to run it. Keep it specific to how the company works.

  • Designated safety coordinator / competent person (1926.20(b)(2), 1926.32(f))
  • Hazard communication program and where SDS sheets are accessed (1910.1200)
  • Required PPE by task and who provides it
  • Emergency action plan: nearest hospital, evacuation, injury reporting, severe weather (1926.35)
  • Posted emergency contacts at each active site (1926.50(f))
  • Trade-specific plans: silica exposure control (1926.1153(g)), fall protection, respiratory protection (1910.134)

Site Safety Walks and Inspections

OSHA 1926.20(b)(2) requires frequent and regular jobsite inspections by a competent person. Document them on the Site Safety Inspection Form as evidence of an active program.

  • Walk the site on a regular cadence, not just after a problem
  • The walker is the competent person: can identify hazards and is authorized to correct them (1926.32(f))
  • Log the hazard, the corrective action, and the date closed

Training and Certifications

Equipment without training does not meet the requirement. Keep records of who is trained and when.

  • First aid / CPR (1926.50(c)): a trained responder on site where no medical facility is near; Red Cross, AHA, or equivalent, renewed about every two years.
  • Respirator fit test and medical evaluation (1910.134(e), (f)): medical evaluation, then a fit test for the specific make, model, and size; refit at least annually; clean-shaven seal area.
  • Silica awareness (1926.1153(i)): for cutting, grinding, or drilling concrete, masonry, tile, or stone; covers hazards, controls, and Table 1.
  • Ladder safety (1926.1060): competent-person training; 4:1 setup and 3 feet above the landing.
  • Safety coordinator / competent person (1926.20(b), 1926.32(f)): runs the program, inspects, and has authority to stop work. Many standards each require a competent person.

High-Risk Activities (OSHA Focus Areas)

The activities OSHA inspects hardest on a remodel. Each one has a specific standard behind it; know the rule and you can run the work and answer for it on site.

Construction Safety Basics (the Focus Four)

OSHA traces most construction deaths to four hazard groups; cover these and you have covered the orientation basics every new hand needs.

  • Falls are the number-one killer: protect edges, holes, and roofs at 6 feet (1926.501); see Fall Protection below
  • Struck-by: vehicles, swinging loads, flying debris, falling tools; stay out of travel and swing paths and tie off overhead material
  • Caught-in / between: trench cave-ins, unguarded moving parts, pinch points between equipment and a fixed object
  • Electrocution: overhead and buried lines, ungrounded tools, missing GFCI; verify dead before opening anything (1926.416)
  • Good housekeeping and engineering controls remove most of these hazards before PPE is even needed

Aerial Lift Safety

Scissor lifts and boom lifts fall under OSHA 1926.453 and the ANSI A92 family; only trained, authorized operators run them.

  • Look out for electrical hazards and power lines; keep the lift and basket clear of overhead lines
  • Operate only on firm, even terrain; watch the work zone for holes and drop-offs
  • Wear a body harness with a lanyard tied to the manufacturer's anchor in a boom lift (1926.453(b)(2)(v))
  • Obey the maximum rated load; never exceed it
  • Never work or stand underneath the basket
  • Run a pre-operation inspection before every use:
    • Fluid levels: oil, hydraulic fluid, coolant
    • Wheels and tires
    • Battery and charger
    • Brakes and steering
    • Controls work (upper and lower)
    • Fire extinguisher in place
    • Hoses and cords undamaged
    • Locking pins in place

Confined Spaces

Attics and crawl spaces commonly qualify as confined spaces under OSHA's construction rule (1926.1203, Subpart AA): big enough to enter, limited entry/exit, and not designed for continuous occupancy.

  • Hazards: poor air quality, fire and physical hazards, heat and cold, low visibility, electrical hazards
  • Mitigation: respirators, eye protection, lights, gloves, suits; a fire extinguisher present
  • A permit-required space needs a written entry permit, atmospheric testing, and an attendant outside (1926.1204, 1926.1209). Use the Confined Space Entry Permit
  • Test the air and record the results before anyone enters
  • If the air is not acceptable, supply an airline (supplied-air) respirator or mechanically ventilate the space until it is

Fall Protection

The most-cited construction standard. Conventional fall protection is required at 6 feet on a remodel (1926.501(b)(1)).

  • Protect open sides and edges at 6 ft with guardrails, a personal fall arrest system, or safety nets (1926.502)
  • Roof work: residential framing/sheathing can use a written fall protection plan only where conventional systems are infeasible (1926.501(b)(13)); low-slope roofs use a warning line and a system at the edge (501(b)(10))
  • Cover and mark every floor and roof hole the moment it is cut (501(b)(4))
  • Inspect harnesses and lanyards before each use; pull anything with cut webbing, deployed indicators, or corroded hardware
  • Train each exposed worker to recognize fall hazards and use the systems (1926.503)

Crystalline Silica

Cutting, grinding, or drilling concrete, masonry, tile, brick, or stone releases respirable silica (1926.1153).

  • Follow Table 1: water-fed (wet) cutting or a tool-mounted dust shroud with a HEPA vacuum is the default control
  • A P100 respirator where Table 1 calls for one or where controls do not hold exposure down
  • A written exposure control plan on site (1153(g)) and worker training (1153(i))
  • Never dry-sweep silica dust; HEPA vacuum or wet methods only

Dump Truck and Heavy Equipment

Motor vehicles and equipment on site fall under OSHA (1926.601, 1926.602); the struck-by and caught-between hazards are what hurt people.

  • Use a spotter and a working backup alarm when backing; keep crew out of the swing and travel path
  • Never stand or work under a raised dump bed without the body prop engaged
  • Check overhead clearance before raising a bed; keep it well clear of power lines
  • Inspect the vehicle before use: brakes, lights, alarm, tires, hydraulics; wear the seat belt
  • Stand clear of the bed and tailgate during dumping; watch for tip-over on soft or uneven ground

Tool and Equipment Safety

Tools and ladders are everyday work, which is exactly why they show up in citations. The key points from the OSHA tool standards (29 CFR 1926 Subpart I for tools, Subpart L for scaffolds, Subpart X for ladders), condensed.

Hand and Power Tools (general)

  • Keep guards in place; never remove or wedge back a blade guard (1926.300(b))
  • Inspect before use; tag and pull damaged tools and frayed cords out of service
  • Double-insulated or grounded tools only; on a remodel run them on GFCI (1926.404(b)(1))
  • Right tool for the job; don't use undersized cords or the wrong blade
  • Disconnect power before changing blades, bits, or accessories
  • Eye protection always; add hearing and a dust mask for loud or dusty cutting
  • Carry by the handle, not the cord or hose; never yank a cord from the outlet

Pneumatic Tools and Nail Guns

  • Secure the hose to the tool with a clip or retainer so the line can't whip loose (1926.302(b)(1))
  • Do not exceed the tool's rated air pressure; check the hose rating too
  • Run a safety clip / muzzle so the tool only fires against the work
  • Full sequential trigger for placement work; it cuts double-fires versus bump (contact) trip
  • Never point a nailer at anyone; never carry it with a finger on the trigger
  • Disconnect the air before clearing a jam, loading, or leaving it down
  • Keep your free hand clear of the line of fire; watch for through-nails and blow-outs at edges
  • Eye and hearing protection; never use compressed air to blow off clothing or skin

Power Saws

  • Lower guard must spring back and fully cover the blade when not cutting (1926.304)
  • Let the blade reach full speed before it touches the material
  • Support the work so the cut doesn't pinch the blade and kick back
  • Keep both hands on the saw; never reach under or behind the material
  • Set blade depth just past the stock; correct blade for the material
  • Unplug to change blades; don't lock or hold the guard open
  • Wet-cut or vacuum-shroud masonry and tile to control silica (1926.1153)

Grinding and Abrasive Wheels

  • Match wheel rating to the tool's rpm; a wheel run too fast can burst (1926.303)
  • Ring-test bonded wheels for cracks before mounting
  • Keep the wheel guard on; tongue guard within 1/4 in., work rest within 1/8 in. on bench grinders
  • Stand to the side and let a new wheel run up to speed before use
  • Face shield over safety glasses; the sparks and fragments carry
  • Don't grind on the side of a wheel made only for edge work

Ladders

  • Extension ladders at a 4:1 pitch (1 ft out per 4 ft up) (1926.1053(b)(5))
  • Side rails extend 3 ft above the landing, or grab the structure at the top
  • Tie off or foot the top; set on firm, level footing
  • Three points of contact; face the ladder going up and down
  • Don't stand on the top cap or top step of a stepladder (1926.1053(b)(13))
  • Keep your belt buckle between the rails; reposition, don't overreach
  • Inspect for cracked rails, bent rungs, missing feet; tag bad ladders out of service
  • Keep metal ladders away from live electrical work

Scaffolds

  • A competent person inspects the scaffold before each shift (1926.451(f)(7))
  • Set on base plates and mud sills on firm ground; plumb and level
  • Fully plank the platform; minimal gaps, planks cleated or overlapped
  • Guardrails (top rail, mid rail) or fall arrest at 10 ft (1926.451(g))
  • Free-standing height no more than 4 times the minimum base width unless tied or braced
  • Safe access: a ladder or stair tower, not the cross braces
  • Keep it clear of power lines and don't overload it past its rating
