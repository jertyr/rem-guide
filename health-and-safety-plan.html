Construction Safety & Health Management System

Accident Prevention Program for Dexter Builders, 8820 Jackson Road, Dexter, MI · (734) 426-3852
Last revised: 06/08/2026 (update committee roster and owner signature each year)

How to use this plan. This is both the company accident prevention program required by 29 CFR 1926.20(b) and MIOSHA Part 1 and a training reference. Use it three ways: walk new hires through it at orientation; pull the shaded Training point boxes for weekly toolbox talks; and open the relevant written program (silica, fall protection, HazCom) before a job that involves that hazard. Every regulated section cites the standard so the foreman on site can speak to it.

1. Management Commitment and Planning

Safety and Health Policy

  • No job or task is more important than worker health and safety. If a job cannot be done safely, it will not be done.
  • Every procedure must be a safe procedure. Shortcuts in safe procedures by foremen or workers will not be tolerated.
  • If a worker observes an unsafe condition, they are expected to report it immediately to their supervisor.
  • Management provides visible, ongoing commitment, resources, and leadership to implement this Safety and Health Management System (SHMS), and acknowledges the importance of a positive safety culture built on employee involvement and effective policies.

Signature of Owner / President

Objectives

  • The project manager is responsible for the health and safety of all employees and subcontractors on their project.
  • Plan for safety before each job and each new task using a written Job Safety Analysis (JSA).
  • Make regular jobsite safety inspections and conduct health monitoring.
  • Follow safety procedures and rules, provide ongoing training, and enforce rules with appropriate discipline.

Designated Safety Coordinator (Competent Person)

Dexter Builders designates the project manager of each jobsite to coordinate, implement, and administer the SHMS. This is the OSHA "competent person": able to identify existing and predictable hazards and authorized to take prompt corrective action (1926.32(f)). The coordinator will:

  • Understand job hazards and how to eliminate them; conduct or assist with the JSA.
  • Assure compliance with MIOSHA construction safety and health standards.
  • Conduct regular jobsite inspections and establish safety procedures.
  • Coordinate training and run Toolbox / Five-Minute Safety Talks.
  • Maintain documentation of training, inspections, injuries and illnesses, and other records.
  • Participate in accident investigations and corrective actions, and involve employees in the SHMS.
Training point. Several standards each require their own competent person (silica, excavation, scaffolds, fall protection). One person can hold multiple roles, but they must be trained for each. Name the competent person at the start of every job.

Supervisor Responsibility, Committee, and Responding to Issues

  • Supervisors set the tone; their attitude drives acceptance of safe work practices.
  • The safety and health committee includes management and employee representatives and meets regularly with defined goals, a formal agenda, posted minutes, and documented action items. Current members: update annually (2016 roster on file).
  • Management takes prompt, consistent action on safety issues. Supervisors review, investigate, and act on all employee reports of hazards.

2. Employee Involvement

  • Committee: participates in implementing the SHMS, addresses issues, and involves employees in problem solving.
  • Inspections: employees take part in regular safety inspections to identify hazardous conditions and unsafe practices.
  • Suggestions: employees are encouraged to submit safety suggestions to improve a process or prevent an accident.
  • Participation: employees provide input on safety products, procedures, and training.

3. Worksite Analysis

We use systematic actions to recognize and understand jobsite hazards:

  • Job Safety Analysis (JSA) for each task. See the sample JSA form on file.
  • Comprehensive hazard surveys (insurance inspections, MIOSHA on-site consultations).
  • Hazard analysis of workplace changes (new equipment, processes, materials), performed by the project manager.
  • Regular site inspections by employees and management.
  • Employee reports of hazards.
  • Accident, incident, and near-miss investigations with corrective action and follow-up.
  • Injury and illness trend analysis, PPE assessment, and identification of confined spaces and energy sources.

Job Safety Analysis (JSA)

The JSA is the core planning tool. It has three parts:

  1. Break the job into the specific steps it takes to complete.
  2. List the hazards in each step.
  3. Write how each hazard will be eliminated or controlled.
Training point. The JSA is where silica, fall, and electrical hazards get caught before work starts. Required PPE for the task is recorded at the top of the JSA form, so the crew knows what to wear before they pick up a tool.

Accident / Incident Investigation

  • All accidents, no matter how minor, are reported to the foreman immediately; foremen report to the Safety Coordinator as soon as possible.
  • The foreman completes an initial written investigation the day of the accident when possible; involved workers and witnesses provide written statements.
  • The Safety Coordinator completes a full investigation to find root cause and corrective actions. The goal is to find the cause, not fault.
  • Near misses are reported and corrected to prevent recurrence.

4. Hazard Prevention and Control

Hazards are controlled in this order of preference:

  1. Remove the hazard.
  2. Guard against it as required by MIOSHA.
  3. Provide PPE and enforce its use.
  4. Train workers in safe work practices.
  5. Coordinate protection of workers across other contractors.
  • Daily jobsite inspections are conducted; hazards are documented, reviewed, and corrected promptly, and records are kept.
  • A vehicle and equipment maintenance schedule is kept with logs of work performed.
  • Required written programs are maintained: lockout/tagout, respiratory protection, hazard communication, confined space, silica, asbestos, lead, and powered industrial truck permits.

5. Personal Protective Equipment

PPE is required whenever a hazard is present (1926.95). The on-site set and the printable van copy are on the Jobsite Safety page and the PPE and Safety Equipment Checklist.

  • Head: hard hats meeting ANSI Z89.1 worn on jobsites (1926.100). Company policy is hard hats on whenever overhead work is possible; a blanket site policy avoids the judgment call.
  • Eyes: Z87-rated safety glasses for flying particles, dust, chemicals, glare, or arcing (1926.102).
  • Hearing: plugs or muffs where noise reaches a 90 dBA 8-hour average; protection made available at 85 dBA (1926.101, 1926.52).
  • Respiratory: N95 for general dust; half- or full-face P100 for silica, lead, and mold. See Respiratory Protection below.
  • Feet: leather work boots; safety-toe (ASTM F2413) where there is risk of falling or rolling objects or punctures (1926.96).
  • Hands / body: gloves and protective clothing matched to the hazard (cut, chemical, Tyvek for lead or heavy dust).
  • Fall protection: harness and lanyard as required (see Fall Protection).
  • High-visibility: vests near traffic and moving equipment.

6. Safety and Health Rules

All safety rules must be obeyed; failure results in discipline. Key rules:

  • Watch where you walk; do not run; keep your mind on the work. Keep the work area free of rubbish and debris.
  • No illegal drugs or alcohol during working hours. No horseplay or distracting other workers.
  • Do not use compressed air to blow dust from clothes, hair, or hands.
  • Lift with the legs, not the back; get help with heavy loads.
  • Know where fire extinguishers are and how to use them.
  • Stay back at least 10 feet from power lines; do not store materials under them.
  • Only operators ride equipment; never walk within the swing radius of counterweights; barricade danger areas and crane zones.
  • Do not operate power tools until trained and authorized; never remove a guard or safety device.
  • Lock out and tag out energy before servicing equipment.
  • Do not throw anything from heights; someone may be below. Open fires are prohibited.

Ladders and Scaffolds

  • Set ladders at the 4-to-1 ratio: one foot of base for every four feet of height (1926.1053).
  • Extension ladders extend three feet above the landing or roofline; secure or have a handhold.
  • Do not stand on the top or top step of a stepladder (1926.1053(b)(13)). Tag and remove defective ladders from service; keep ladder bases clear.
  • Build scaffolds per manufacturer and MIOSHA Part 12; scaffolds over 10 feet have guardrails on all open sides; plank properly lapped and secured.
Toolbox talk. "4 in 1, and 3 feet above the roofline." Have a worker set an extension ladder and check the angle and the 3-foot extension before anyone climbs.

7. Safety Discipline

A four-step system applies when safety rules are not followed:

  1. First violation: oral warning, noted in the file.
  2. Second: written warning, copy to file.
  3. Third: written warning and one-day suspension without pay.
  4. Fourth: written warning and one-week suspension, or termination if warranted.

Zero-tolerance violations may result in termination with no warning, including entering hazardous confined spaces without procedures, failing to use fall protection, and entering unsafe excavations. Both the worker and a supervisor who allows the unsafe act may be terminated. All disciplinary actions are recorded.

8. Emergency Procedures

  • Determine the method of communication at each site (telephone, radio).
  • Post emergency telephone numbers (police, fire, medical) and the jobsite address near the communication station (1926.50(f)). The fillable posting template is on the Jobsite Safety Signage page.
  • Post names of on-site first-aid responders; responders maintain current First Aid / CPR and bloodborne pathogen training.
  • Designate a person to direct emergency crews to the site.
  • Maintain a stocked first aid kit; brief workers on environmental hazards (plants, insects, animals) where present.

9. Lockout / Tagout

Lockout/Tagout protects employees from unintended machine motion or release of stored energy (1926.417). All energy sources must be shut off, de-energized at the source, and locked and tagged out before any work on or around the hazard begins.

10. Confined Space Entry

No employee enters a confined space without authorization (1926 Subpart AA). A confined space is one that is not designed for continuous occupancy, is large enough to enter and work in, and has limited or restricted entry or exit.

  • A qualified person tests the atmosphere with an appropriate detector before entry. Hazardous atmospheres include flammable gas, combustible dust, oxygen below 19.5% or above 23.5%, toxic substances, or engulfment risk.
  • Wear the necessary PPE; provide ventilation with fresh air; an attendant (hole-watch) may be stationed at the entrance.

11. Hazard Communication (SDS / Right to Know)

Hazard communication ensures all workers know about the chemicals they work with and around (1910.1200, adopted for construction by MIOSHA Part 42). The program includes:

  • A written hazard communication program and training on the chemicals the company uses.
  • Safety Data Sheets (SDS), the GHS-format replacement for the old MSDS, readily available on site. Workers must know where to find them and how to read them. Electronic access is acceptable with a reliable backup.
  • Properly labeled containers using the GHS pictogram and signal-word system.
  • Posting the location of SDS and notice when new chemicals arrive on site.
  • Informing other contractors of hazards from chemicals we use around them.
Training point. "MSDS" is the old name. Sheets are now SDS and follow a fixed 16-section GHS format. Common jobsite chemicals needing an SDS: adhesives, solvents, fuels, finishes, sealants, and spray foam.

12. Respirable Crystalline Silica

This written exposure control plan is required by 29 CFR 1926.1153 (MIOSHA Part 690). It was not in the 2016 plan and is the most important addition.

What and why

Respirable crystalline silica is the fine dust released when cutting, grinding, drilling, or breaking concrete, masonry, brick, block, tile, mortar, and stone. The particles are small enough to lodge deep in the lungs and cause silicosis, lung cancer, COPD, and kidney disease. The OSHA permissible exposure limit is 50 micrograms per cubic meter of air as an 8-hour average, with an action level of 25.

Tasks that create silica on our jobs

  • Cutting or grinding concrete, block, brick, pavers, and fiber-cement siding
  • Tuckpointing and surface grinding of mortar
  • Drilling into concrete or masonry
  • Jackhammering or breaking concrete and demolition of masonry
  • Mixing dry mortar, grout, thinset, or concrete
  • Dry sweeping or using compressed air to clean silica dust (prohibited, see housekeeping)

Controls: follow Table 1

For each task on OSHA Table 1, we fully implement the listed dust controls and respirator requirement; doing so means we do not have to measure exposures. The controls we rely on:

TaskControlRespirator
Handheld masonry / cut-off sawIntegrated water feed keeping the blade wetN95 if used > 4 hours/shift outdoors
Handheld grinder (concrete, mortar)Shroud with HEPA-filtered dust collection (M- or H-class vac)N95 (longer durations may need more)
Handheld drill / core drillShroud or hollow bit with HEPA dust collectionNone to N95 per Table 1
Jackhammer / breakerWater spray or HEPA dust collectionN95 outdoors; upgrade indoors
Dry mixing mortar / concreteMix outdoors or use vacuum-controlled hopper; pre-blended bags where possibleN95 / P100 per conditions

Two simple field rules cover most of it: wet it or vacuum it, and never dry-cut silica. Use saws with a working water feed and grinders with a HEPA-shroud vacuum. If a control is not available, stop and call the competent person.

Respirators, housekeeping, medical, training

  • Respirators: where Table 1 calls for one, use at minimum an N95; use half- or full-face P100 for heavier or indoor work. Respirator use triggers the Respiratory Protection program below (medical evaluation and annual fit test).
  • Housekeeping: no dry sweeping and no compressed air for silica dust. Use a HEPA vacuum or wet methods.
  • Medical surveillance: offered to any worker required to wear a respirator for silica tasks 30 or more days a year, including a baseline exam, chest x-ray, and lung-function test, repeated at least every three years.
  • Competent person: a designated competent person makes frequent and regular inspections of silica tasks and controls and can correct problems on the spot.
  • Training: every worker who performs a silica task is trained on the health hazard, the tasks that create exposure, the controls in this plan, the contents of Table 1, and the medical surveillance offered (1926.1153(i)).
  • Recordkeeping: keep training records, any exposure data, and medical records.
Toolbox talk. Show the saw water feed and the grinder HEPA shroud. Ask: which of today's tasks make silica, and what is the control for each? If there is no control, the task does not start.

13. Respiratory Protection

Respirator use on our jobs is governed by 29 CFR 1910.134 (construction defers to it).

  • Selection: N95 filtering facepiece for general and nuisance dust; half- or full-face respirator with P100 cartridges (99.97%, oil-proof) for silica, lead, mold, and heavy dust.
  • Medical evaluation: required before a worker is fit tested or required to wear a tight-fitting respirator.
  • Fit testing: required before first use and at least annually, for the specific make, model, and size. The seal area must be clean-shaven.
  • Voluntary use: workers who choose to wear an N95 when not required are still given Appendix D of 1910.134.
  • Care: inspect before use; clean, store, and replace cartridges per manufacturer instructions.

14. Fall Protection

Fall protection is required whenever working at six feet or above (1926.501), provided by one or more of:

  • Guardrails;
  • Hole covers (covered and marked) (1926.501(b)(4));
  • Safety nets;
  • Personal fall arrest system (harness and lanyard). 100% tie-off means the harness and lanyard stay connected to anchorage at all times.
Toolbox talk. "6 feet up: guardrail, harness, or net." Walk the deck and point out every leading edge and every floor or roof opening before work starts.

15. Electrical Safety

Two issues drive electrical safety: power lines, and temporary and permanent wiring and equipment (1926.404).

  • Stay at least 10 feet back from power lines in all directions; do not store materials under them; mark them with warning signs.
  • Use GFCI protection whenever plugging into temporary power or using an extension cord (1926.404(b)(1)).
  • Remove from service any extension or trailing cord with a missing ground prong or cuts. Use three-prong cords only. Keep cords out of wet areas and protected in doorways.
  • Do not operate wet power tools. Assume all wires and boxes are live until verified dead with a tester. Leave wiring and repairs to a qualified electrician.
  • Keep materials at least 3 feet clear of electrical panels.

16. Excavation Safety

Pre-job planning is vital; safety cannot be improvised as work progresses. A qualified person addresses the following (1926 Subpart P, MIOSHA Part 9):

  • Evaluate soil and select protective systems. Protective systems are required at 5 feet deep, or shallower if conditions are hazardous.
  • Protective systems include trench boxes, shoring, benching, and sloping.
  • Inspect daily at the start of each shift, after rain, and after any hazard-increasing event.
  • Call MISS DIG to locate underground lines at least 3 days before excavating.
  • Plan traffic control where needed (Michigan MMUTC Part 6).
  • Test for low oxygen and hazardous gases, especially with engine-driven equipment running or contaminated soil.
  • Provide safe access: a ladder within 25 feet of workers when the excavation is 4 feet or deeper. Control water accumulation. Keep excavations open the minimum time needed.

17. Safety and Health Training

Training meets MIOSHA requirements and is evaluated continuously. The program includes:

  • New employee orientation: the SHMS, safe work practices, and expectations, using this plan as the reference.
  • Hazard-based training: after inspecting a job, the project manager identifies hazards and the Safety Coordinator provides the needed training; length and detail match the severity of the hazard and MIOSHA requirements.
  • Toolbox talks: conducted regularly (weekly or daily), covering the day's safe work practices, worker concerns, and a brief refresher on a relevant topic. Use the shaded Training point and Toolbox talk boxes in this plan as ready-made topics.
  • Certifications to keep current: First Aid / CPR, respirator medical evaluation and fit test, silica awareness, and ladder safety. Track who is trained and when.
  • Records: maintained for all sessions with topics covered and names of workers trained.
Training point. A documented toolbox talk is the cheapest proof of an active program. Date it, list the topic, and pass it around for signatures.

18. Safety and Health Work Observations

Supervisors or designated observers periodically observe work to confirm two things: the employee has the knowledge to perform the work as trained, and is actually performing it that way. Findings feed back into coaching and training.
