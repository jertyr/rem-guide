Mold Remediation

Small-scale mold cleanup is a routine part of remodeling. Pull back drywall on a window that's been leaking and you may find it. This page covers how to think about the scope of the problem, set up safely, and treat affected framing and drywall. We are not mold remediation professionals; for larger infestations, ongoing moisture problems, or HVAC contamination, consult a certified remediator.

Quick Reference

  • Fix the moisture source first. Treating mold on wet material may not be effective
  • For small areas (the EPA suggests under about 10 sq ft, roughly a 3 ft by 3 ft patch, in A Brief Guide to Mold, Moisture, and Your Home), you may be able to handle cleanup yourself with proper PPE and containment
  • For larger areas, persistent or recurring moisture, mold in HVAC ducts, or visible growth across multiple rooms, consider hiring a certified remediation professional
  • Always read product labels and follow manufacturer instructions. Mold-killing products are serious chemicals and must be used and handled appropriately
  • Set up containment before disturbing mold. Cutting, scraping, or vacuuming releases spores into the air
  • See the Jobsite Protection page for general dust walls, plastic, and tape setup
  • Kill, then remove staining if needed, then encapsulate. These are separate steps and separate products

PPE and Containment

  • PPE
    • Minimum for small cleanup: N95 respirator, nitrile gloves, safety glasses
    • For anything over 10 sq ft or heavy growth: half-face respirator with P100 filters, disposable Tyvek suit, nitrile gloves taped at the cuffs
    • Do not use a dust mask. Mold spores pass through them
  • Containment
    • Seal HVAC registers and returns in the work area before starting; tape 6-mil poly over them
    • For larger areas: build a poly barrier around the work zone with a zipper entry. Run a negative air machine exhausting outside (not into another room or attic) to keep spore count low in adjacent spaces
    • Bag demo material in 6-mil poly bags and seal before moving through the house

Process

  • Step 1: Find and Fix the Source
    • Identify where the water came from: failed window flashing, missing caulk, roof leak, plumbing leak, condensation
    • Repair the source before doing anything else. Treating mold on wet material may not be effective
    • Confirm the assembly is dry before treating. Wood at or above roughly 19% moisture content can continue to support fungal growth (USDA Forest Products Laboratory Wood Handbook, Ch. 14). A pin-type moisture meter is the simplest way to verify the framing has dried before applying any biocide
  • Step 2: Demo Affected Material
    • Remove drywall and insulation with visible or suspected mold growth. These are not worth treating in place; demo and replace
    • For framing with surface mold only, treatment in place is acceptable if the wood is structurally sound and fully dry
    • Bag demo debris in 6-mil poly bags and seal before moving through occupied spaces
    • HEPA-vacuum surfaces after demo to remove loose spore load before applying any liquid product
  • Step 3: Kill
    • Apply an EPA-registered biocide to all affected framing and sheathing surfaces
    • Read the product label and SDS before opening the container. These are serious chemicals; mixing, dwell times, ventilation, and PPE all vary by product and must be followed
    • See Products section below for options
    • For heavy surface growth, scrub with a stiff brush after the labeled dwell time, then HEPA-vac, then apply a second coat
  • Step 4: Remove Staining (if needed)
    • Staining on drywall paper or wood grain after killing is cosmetic but may need to be addressed before priming
    • RMR-86 (sodium hypochlorite) is the standard stain remover for this step. It works in 15–60 seconds on surface stains but is not a mold killer on its own
    • Use with heavy ventilation and full PPE; wear splash goggles
    • Do not use RMR-86 on materials you haven't already killed with a registered biocide
  • Step 5: Dry
    • After wet treatment products, allow framing to fully dry before encapsulating. Sealing damp wood traps moisture and promotes regrowth. Use fans and dehumidifiers and verify the framing has dried with a moisture meter before proceeding
  • Step 6: Encapsulate
    • Apply an antimicrobial encapsulant coating to treated framing and sheathing before closing the wall
    • This creates a long-term barrier that inhibits future growth if minor moisture recurs
    • Foster 40-25 Full Defense is the correct product for porous surfaces (framing, sheathing, OSB). Covers 300 sq ft per gallon
    • Zinsser Mold Killing Primer is labeled for non-porous surfaces only and is not approved as a professional encapsulant for exposed framing or drywall in humid conditions. Use it as a primer on finished painted drywall you are keeping, not on raw wood
  • Step 7: Rebuild
    • Once encapsulant is cured, reinstall insulation, drywall, and any trim
    • Photograph completed remediation before closing the wall for documentation
    • Replace window flashing, caulk, or weatherstripping as part of the rebuild — not as a separate punch item

Products

Read the label and SDS for any product below before use. Active ingredients, dwell times, ventilation requirements, and PPE differ between products and must be followed.

Kill-First (Biocides / Disinfectants)

  • Concrobium Mold Control — Bleach-free biocide. Active chemistry (sodium carbonate/trisodium phosphate) crushes mold cells as it dries and leaves an antimicrobial barrier. Apply by pump sprayer, let dry fully, then scrub or HEPA-vac. A common single-product DIY option
  • Vital Oxide (EPA Reg. #82972-1) — Hospital disinfectant used by professional remediation companies. Active ingredient is chlorine dioxide; penetrates porous and non-porous surfaces including drywall, wood framing, concrete, and carpet. No rinse required, no VOCs, non-corrosive
  • Benefect Botanical Decon 30 — EPA-registered botanical disinfectant (thyme oil based) used in water damage restoration. This is a second-step disinfectant after physical removal, not a stain remover
  • Foster 40-80 First Defense (EPA Reg. #6836-152) — Hospital-grade EPA-registered fungicide, cleaner, disinfectant, and deodorizer in one. Designed for water damage restoration on porous, semi-porous, and non-porous surfaces including drywall, subfloors, framing, and trim
  • RMR-141 — EPA-registered quat-ammonium disinfectant. Kills mold on porous surfaces including drywall and wood; creates a protective barrier against regrowth. This is the "kill" step in the RMR two-step system

Stain Removal (After Kill Step Only)

  • RMR-86 — Sodium hypochlorite (bleach-based) stain eliminator. Not an EPA-registered mold killer. Use it after a biocide has already killed the mold, to remove the dark staining left in drywall paper or wood grain. Use with heavy ventilation and full PPE

Encapsulation / Final Coat

  • Foster 40-25 Full Defense Fungicidal Protective Coating — EPA-registered antimicrobial encapsulant for porous surfaces after remediation. Breathable, designed to prevent long-term mold regrowth on exposed framing or drywall
  • Zinsser Mold Killing Primer (EPA Reg. #87469-1) — Antimicrobial primer that kills residual surface mold and seals before topcoat. Labeled for non-porous surfaces only; use it as a primer on finished painted drywall you are keeping, not as an encapsulant on raw wood or in humid conditions

Validated pro workflow for a 100 sq ft drywall remediation: RMR-141 or Vital Oxide to kill, RMR-86 if staining is severe, Foster 40-25 to encapsulate. For simpler DIY scope: Concrobium in a pump sprayer handles kill and prevention in one step without bleach chemistry.

Best Practices

  • Kill and encapsulate are separate steps that require separate products. Priming over live mold, or using a stain remover as a biocide, will not stop regrowth
  • The moisture source is the actual problem. Mold is a symptom. Every remediation starts with finding and fixing the leak or condensation issue
  • If you open a wall and the mold is only on the drywall paper, not the framing, the exposure was likely short-lived. If framing is heavily colonized or structurally compromised, extend the scope
  • HEPA-vac before you spray. Applying a liquid biocide to a heavy spore load without removing loose material first just moves the problem
  • Document everything with photos: the discovery, the demo, the framing condition, the treatment, and the encapsulant coat before closing the wall. This protects you if a callback comes in six months
  • Never cover mold with new drywall without treating the framing. The mold is still viable behind the paper and will continue growing
  • Concrobium is the safest product for an occupied home with no relocation. No bleach, no VOCs, no mandatory ventilation. For unoccupied spaces or vacant demo sites, stronger chemistry (Vital Oxide, Foster 40-80) is appropriate

Client Communication

  • Notify the client before opening any wall where mold is suspected. Verbal is fine but follow up in writing
  • Explain that mold discovery during demo can extend the scope and timeline. Most clients accept this if they understand why the source has to be fixed and why new material can't go in wet or contaminated framing
  • Clarify what you are doing (surface mold removal on framing) vs. what requires a certified remediator (large-scale infestation, HVAC contamination). Clients often conflate the two after seeing news coverage of black mold
  • After remediation, confirm in writing that affected material was removed or treated, the moisture source was repaired, and the encapsulant was applied before closing the wall
